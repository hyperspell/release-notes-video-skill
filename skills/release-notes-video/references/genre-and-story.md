# Genre and story

## Specific sound, recognizable starting point

Name the era, instruments, groove, production and vocal approach. “Late-1990s boy-band pop with staccato strings and stacked harmonies” gives a producer more direction than “pop.” Specificity need not mean obscurity: choose references the intended audience can recognize, then make the execution precise.

The genre shapes the whole episode: footage, lighting, typography, transitions, caption placement and the way product screens enter the scene. Use one strong format joke, executed consistently, rather than a pile of unrelated effects.

Pitch three genuinely different options. Each should include:

- A recognizable artist, song or scene reference for discussion.
- Musical instructions in plain production language, including how words will remain intelligible.
- A title or hook idea and one sample couplet.
- An opening image, narrator, visual world and recurring joke.
- Why this form fits the chosen update, plus its main tradeoff.

Keep artist/song names out of a generation prompt when the service disallows them. Do not copy their lyrics or melodies. Use original characters and approved reference assets rather than assuming a likeness or brand is free to reuse.

## Starting directions

These are prompts for taste, not fixed scripts or claims about model capabilities:

| Musical direction | Sound and world | Useful for |
|---|---|---|
| 1980s monster funk-pop | Tight synth bass, rhythm guitar, brass stabs, theatrical narration; a haunted office dance floor | A Halloween hero launch |
| 1990s instructional VHS | Smooth electric piano, sax, reassuring spoken verses and an oversized sung hook; beige training-room sets | A change that needs explaining |
| Turn-of-the-millennium boy band | Syncopated pop, staccato strings, stacked harmonies, an unnecessary key change; rain, coordinated outfits | Team or agent collaboration |
| Early-2000s emo | Palm-muted guitars, indignant tenor, explosive chorus; basement shows and handwritten titles | An emotionally excessive workplace pain point |
| Victorian comic operetta | Pit orchestra, clear baritone patter, grand ensemble endings; painted flats and playbills | Several concise updates |
| 1960s spy-film theme | Baritone guitar, brass, sweeping strings and a belting lead; dossiers and geometric silhouettes | A genuinely relevant permissions or control feature |
| 1980s workout tape | Drum machines, bright synths, instructor verses and sung refrain; leg warmers and a relentlessly earnest studio | A step-by-step workflow |
| 1970s educational cartoon | Piano, upright bass, conversational vocals; simple diagrams and a narrator with a job to explain | A feature with a clear causal story |
| Late-1990s big beat | Breakbeats, distorted bass and an original vocal hook; camcorder choreography | A high-energy palate cleanser |
| Haunted early-animation jazz | Muted brass, clarinet, upright bass, elastic movement; impossible objects played completely straight | An audience that likes unusual visual comedy |

## Build an episode, not a mood board

Keep a private history of genres and structural devices. Avoid repeating the recent musical family or rebuilding the same cold open with a different costume. A small callback can reward repeat viewers without becoming the whole structure.

The opening should establish the update's context and give the viewer something striking in the first seconds. Do not spend a quarter of a short video explaining an unfamiliar mascot. A recognizable musical reference, a clear problem and one absurd image can do the job together.

Let the genre carry jokes about workplace conventions. Do not build the humor around a culture, ethnicity, religion or accent. Intentional AI absurdity can be funny; unreadable text, inconsistent faces and accidental broken anatomy usually are not.

Lock the direction with the user before paid generation. The release list may change which form works best, so avoid a fully fixed plot before the actual story is chosen.
